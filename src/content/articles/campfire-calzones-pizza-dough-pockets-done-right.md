<p>The smell of dough hitting a hot cast iron skillet is one of those campfire moments that turns a simple trip into something memorable. There's a sizzle, a puff of steam, and then that golden-brown crust that crackles when you bite into it. Calzones are the perfect camp dinner—they're portable, customizable, and you can cook them over just about any heat source you've got.</p>
<figure><img src="https://cdn.midjourney.com/dfc844f9-cf07-4777-9851-afca8924a074/0_1_640_N.webp" alt="Folding and sealing a pizza dough calzone on a floured surface" /></figure>


<p>The trick is getting the dough to cook through without burning the outside. I've burned my share of crusts learning this. The secret? Medium heat and a little patience. Let the coals die down to a steady glow, or keep your camp stove on medium-low. You want a gentle sizzle, not a raging fire.</p>

<h2>Start with Good Dough</h2>

<p>Store-bought pizza dough works great. Look for the refrigerated kind at the grocery store—it's usually in the deli section. Let it come to room temperature for about 30 minutes before you roll it. Cold dough fights back; warm dough is forgiving.</p>

<p>If you're feeling ambitious, you can make your own dough at home and bring it in a zip-top bag. Just punch it down and keep it cool in the cooler until you're ready. Homemade dough with a little olive oil in the mix tends to brown beautifully over fire.</p>

<h2>Roll and Fill: The Right Ratio</h2>

<p>Divide your dough into four equal balls. Roll each into a 6-7 inch circle, about a quarter-inch thick. Don't go thinner—you need structure to hold the filling. A rolling pin works, but an empty wine bottle does the job in a pinch.</p>

<p>Here's the ratio I keep for a perfect calzone: <strong>2 tablespoons of sauce, 1/2 cup of shredded mozzarella, and a dollop of ricotta.</strong> That's it. Any more and you'll have a leaky mess. Spread the sauce on one half, leaving a one-inch border. Add your cheese and any fillings—cooked sausage, pepperoni, sautéed mushrooms, or just cheese for a classic.</p>

<p>Fold the other half over and press the edges with a fork. This seals it and creates that pretty crimped edge. If you're worried about leaks, you can also pinch and roll the edge like a pie crust.</p>

<h2>Brush and Season</h2>

<p>Don't skip the egg wash. Beat one egg with a tablespoon of water and brush it over the top. This gives you that glossy, golden crust. If you don't have eggs, olive oil works—it just won't be as shiny. Sprinkle with Italian seasoning and a little Parmesan before cooking.</p>

<h2>Cooking Methods: Fire, Grill, or Stove</h2>

<h3>Cast Iron Skillet</h3>

<p>This is my go-to. Heat a cast iron skillet over medium heat with a teaspoon of oil. Place the calzones in the skillet—don't crowd them. Cook for 4-6 minutes per side, until deep golden brown. The internal temperature should hit 165°F. If they're browning too fast, move the skillet to a cooler spot on the fire or turn down the stove.</p>

<h3>Grill</h3>

<p>Preheat your grill to medium (350-400°F). Place the calzones directly on the grate, or on a piece of foil for easier flipping. Close the lid and cook 3-4 minutes per side. You'll get those nice char marks and a smoky flavor that you can't replicate indoors.</p>

<h3>Foil Packets</h3>

<p>For a no-fuss option, wrap each calzone in heavy-duty foil. Place them near hot coals, not directly in the flames. Cook 8-10 minutes, flipping once. The crust will be softer, more like a baked calzone, but it's still delicious—and cleanup is a breeze.</p>

<h2>Common Mistakes to Avoid</h2>

<ul>
    <li><strong>Overstuffing.</strong> It's tempting, but too much filling makes it impossible to seal. Keep to the ratio.</li>
    <li><strong>Too high heat.</strong> You'll burn the outside and leave the inside doughy. Medium heat is your friend.</li>
    <li><strong>Skipping the rest.</strong> Let the calzones sit for 2-3 minutes after cooking. The filling is molten lava and will burn your mouth.</li>
</ul>

<h2>Make-Ahead and Camping Tips</h2>

<p>You can assemble these at home and keep them in the cooler, separated by parchment paper. They'll hold for a day or two. When you're ready, just cook them as directed. If you're using raw meat, make sure it's fully cooked before adding—no one wants a food-safety surprise at camp.</p>

<p>Keep perishable fillings cold until you're ready to assemble. And always have a way to put out the fire—a bucket of water or a fire extinguisher—within reach.</p>

<h2>Final Thoughts</h2>

<p>Calzones are one of those camp meals that feel like a treat but are actually pretty simple. Once you get the hang of the heat and the sealing, you can experiment with all kinds of fillings—pesto and chicken, BBQ pork, or just a mountain of cheese. The fire does the work; you just have to watch the color.</p>

<p>So next time you're planning a camp menu, give these a shot. They're the kind of dinner that makes everyone lean in a little closer to the fire, waiting for that first bite.</p>

<h2>Frequently Asked Questions</h2>
<h3>Can I use pre-made pizza dough that's been frozen?</h3>
<p>Yes. Thaw it overnight in the cooler or a few hours at room temp. Just make sure it's fully thawed before rolling—cold dough will tear. If it's too sticky, dust with flour.</p>
<h3>What's the best way to cook calzones over a campfire?</h3>
<p>Use a cast iron skillet with a lid, or make foil packets. For the skillet, keep the heat medium and turn often. For foil, place near coals, not flames. Both work; the skillet gives a crispier crust.</p>
<h3>How do I prevent the calzones from getting soggy?</h3>
<p>Don't overstuff with sauce—use just 2 tablespoons per calzone. Also, make sure your fillings are dry (pat cooked meats dry). And don't skip the egg wash; it creates a barrier that keeps the dough crisp.</p>
