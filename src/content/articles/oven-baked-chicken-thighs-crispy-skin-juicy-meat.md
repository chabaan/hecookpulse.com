<p>The first time you pull a sheet of chicken thighs out of a 425°F oven, the skin should crackle like a campfire ember. That sound is the promise of everything right: fat rendered to a thin, brittle shell, meat that yields with a gentle pull. Baking thighs isn't complicated, but it rewards a little patience and one crucial step.</p>
<figure><img src="https://cdn.midjourney.com/02310441-30aa-45a1-8b0f-1f6232aa246f/0_1_640_N.webp" alt="Seasoning chicken thighs with spices before baking" /></figure>


<h2>Why Thighs Beat Breasts (For This)</h2>
<p>Chicken thighs have more fat and connective tissue than breasts. That fat is your friend—it bastes the meat from the inside, keeping it moist even if you run a few minutes over. The skin, if treated right, turns into a savory cracker. The trick is to dry it out before it hits the heat.</p>

<h2>The Dry Brine: Your Secret Weapon</h2>
<p>Most recipes tell you to pat the chicken and season it. I go further. I salt the thighs and let them sit uncovered in the fridge for at least an hour, ideally overnight. The salt draws out moisture, then dissolves and gets reabsorbed, seasoning the meat all the way through. The exposed skin dries out, which means less steam, more crisp.</p>
<p>Here's the ratio: 1 teaspoon of kosher salt per pound of chicken. For 2.5 pounds, that's about 1 tablespoon. Don't worry about over-salting—some will fall off. If you're using table salt, cut it to 2 teaspoons.</p>

<h2>The Right Pan and Rack</h2>
<p>Use a rimmed baking sheet. The rim catches the fat that renders out. Place a wire rack on top. This lifts the chicken off the pan, allowing hot air to circulate underneath. No rack? Crumple foil into a few mounds and rest the thighs on those—it works in a pinch, though the rack is better.</p>
<p>If you're cooking over a campfire, skip the rack and use a cast iron skillet. The high sides contain splatter, and the skillet holds heat well. Just know you'll get a softer skin on the bottom—the top will still crisp if you keep the lid off.</p>

<h2>Seasoning That Doesn't Fight the Chicken</h2>
<p>Keep it simple. Garlic powder, smoked paprika, black pepper, and a pinch of thyme. The paprika gives color and a gentle smoke. Garlic powder sticks better than fresh and doesn't burn as fast. Rub the spices on with a little olive oil—just enough to coat, about a tablespoon per 8 thighs.</p>

<h2>Oven Temperature and Timing</h2>
<p>Preheat to 425°F (220°C). That's the sweet spot: hot enough to crisp the skin before the meat overcooks, but not so hot that the spices burn. Bake for 30 minutes, then check. The skin should be deep golden and pulling away from the meat. If it needs more, broil for 2-3 minutes, watching every second—broilers go from perfect to black in a heartbeat.</p>
<p>For campfire cooking, use a Dutch oven with a few coals underneath and a few on top. Aim for about 400°F inside. You'll need 40-50 minutes, rotating the pot halfway. Use an instant-read thermometer to judge doneness, not the clock.</p>

<h2>Doneness: The Number That Matters</h2>
<p>For thighs, target 175°F to 185°F (80°C to 85°C) in the thickest part, away from the bone. That's higher than the 165°F minimum for poultry, but it's what breaks down the collagen and makes the meat tender. At 165°F, thighs can still be chewy. Trust the thermometer, not the color of the juice—clear juice is a good sign, but not a guarantee.</p>

<h2>Rest and Serve</h2>
<p>Let the thighs rest for 5 minutes after they come out. This lets the juices redistribute. If you cut into them immediately, the juices run onto the cutting board and the meat dries out. Five minutes is enough.</p>

<h2>Common Mistakes to Avoid</h2>
<ul>
<li><strong>Skipping the dry brine:</strong> You'll get decent skin, not crackling skin. The extra hour is worth it.</li>
<li><strong>Overcrowding the pan:</strong> If the thighs touch, they steam each other. Leave at least an inch between each piece.</li>
<li><strong>Using a glass pan:</strong> Glass heats unevenly and can shatter under high heat. Stick to metal.</li>
<li><strong>Forgetting to check temperature:</strong> Thighs vary in size. A thermometer is the only reliable way.</li>
</ul>

<h2>Make-Ahead and Camping Tips</h2>
<p>You can dry brine the thighs the night before, then pack them in a cooler with ice. They'll stay cold and ready to bake when you set up camp. If you're cooking over a fire, bring a cast iron skillet and a lid—you can bake in it just like an oven. For a no-fuss camp meal, skip the dry brine and just season generously; you'll still get a great result, just a touch less crispy.</p>

<h2>Takeaway</h2>
<p>Baked chicken thighs are forgiving, flavorful, and hard to ruin if you remember the basics: dry the skin, use high heat, and cook to 175°F. That's the whole game. The rest is just seasoning and a little patience.</p>

<h2>Frequently Asked Questions</h2>
<h3>What temperature should chicken thighs be cooked to?</h3>
<p>For dark meat, aim for 175°F to 185°F (80°C to 85°C). This is higher than the USDA minimum of 165°F, but it breaks down connective tissue and makes the meat tender and juicy, not rubbery.</p>
<h3>Should I bake chicken thighs covered or uncovered?</h3>
<p>Bake them uncovered. Covering traps steam, which softens the skin. Leaving them uncovered lets the skin dry out and crisp up. If you're adding a sauce, apply it in the last 10 minutes to avoid sogginess.</p>
<h3>Can I use boneless, skinless thighs?</h3>
<p>Yes, but adjust the time. Boneless thighs bake faster—about 20-25 minutes at 425°F. They won't get crispy skin, but you can finish them under the broiler for a little char. Check for 165°F minimum, but 175°F is better for tenderness.</p>
