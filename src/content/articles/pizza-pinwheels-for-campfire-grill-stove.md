<p>The first time I made pizza pinwheels at a campsite, I was skeptical. Rolling dough, slicing it into spirals, and trusting a fire to cook them evenly felt like a lot of moving parts. But the moment the bottoms turned golden and the cheese bubbled through the seams, I was hooked. They're portable, fun, and surprisingly forgiving—as long as you keep a few key techniques in mind.</p>
<figure><img src="https://cdn.midjourney.com/ccb869cc-fe31-4dba-b191-77c6360043fa/0_1_640_N.webp" alt="Slicing a rolled pizza dough log into pinwheels on a cutting board." /></figure>


<p>Here's the thing: pizza pinwheels are essentially a rolled pizza, sliced into coin-sized spirals. The dough is the tricky part—it needs to cook all the way through without burning the outside. That's why the method matters more than the ingredients. Whether you're cooking over a campfire, on a grill, or on a camp stove, the same principles apply.</p>

<h2>The Best Dough for Campfire Pinwheels</h2>

<p>I keep it simple with refrigerated pizza crust—it's reliable, doesn't need rising, and rolls out easily. If you prefer homemade, use a basic pizza dough and let it rest at room temperature for 30 minutes before rolling. The key is to roll it into a rectangle about 12x10 inches. Too thick, and the center will be doughy; too thin, and the filling will tear through.</p>

<p>One tip I've learned: use a lightly floured surface and a rolling pin. If you don't have a rolling pin, a clean wine bottle works in a pinch—just wrap it in plastic wrap first. The dough should be about 1/4-inch thick, which gives you a good balance of crispy exterior and soft interior.</p>

<h2>Filling Ratios That Work</h2>

<p>Here's the ratio I stick to: for every 12x10-inch rectangle, use 1/2 cup of sauce, 1 1/2 cups of shredded mozzarella, and 1/4 cup of grated Parmesan. That's enough to coat every bite without making the pinwheels soggy. If you overload the filling, the dough will struggle to seal, and the pinwheels will unroll in the skillet.</p>

<p>Pepperoni is classic, but you can swap in cooked sausage, mushrooms, or even just extra cheese. Just make sure any wet ingredients (like fresh tomatoes) are patted dry, or they'll release moisture and make the dough gummy.</p>

<h2>Rolling and Slicing for Clean Spirals</h2>

<p>Spread the sauce evenly, leaving a 1/2-inch border on one long edge. That bare edge acts as a seal when you roll. Layer the cheese and toppings, then roll tightly from the sauced edge. Pinch the seam to seal—this is where most people get into trouble. If the seam isn't sealed, the pinwheels will burst open during cooking.</p>

<p>Now for slicing: use a sharp knife and wipe it clean between cuts. This keeps the pinwheels round and prevents the dough from dragging. Aim for 1-inch thick slices—any thinner, and they dry out; any thicker, and they take too long to cook through.</p>

<h2>Cooking Over Fire: The Cast Iron Method</h2>

<p>My go-to is a cast iron skillet over medium-hot coals. You want the heat to be around 350°F (175°C)—enough to brown the dough but not scorch it. Place the skillet on a grate or directly on a bed of coals that have died down to a steady glow. If the fire is too hot, the bottoms will burn before the centers cook.</p>

<p>Brush the skillet with a little oil, then arrange the pinwheels cut-side down. Give them room to expand—they'll puff up as they cook. After 4-5 minutes, check the bottoms; they should be deep golden brown. Flip each one with tongs and cook another 4-5 minutes. The dough is done when it reaches an internal temperature of 200°F (93°C)—if you don't have a thermometer, tap the side of a pinwheel; it should sound hollow.</p>

<h3>Grill Variation</h3>

<p>On a gas or charcoal grill, set up a two-zone fire: one side hot, one side cooler. Sear the pinwheels on the hot side for 2 minutes per side, then move them to the cooler side and cover the grill for 5-7 minutes to finish cooking. This gives you the best of both worlds—crispy exterior and fully cooked center.</p>

<h3>Camp Stove Method</h3>

<p>If you're using a camp stove, a cast iron skillet on medium heat works perfectly. The trick is to be patient—don't crank the heat to high. You'll get a nice even browning in about the same time as the fire method. If the stove has hot spots, rotate the skillet occasionally.</p>

<h2>Foil Packet Backup Plan</h2>

<p>For a no-fuss option, make foil packets. Place the pinwheels on a large sheet of heavy-duty foil, brush with olive oil, and wrap tightly. Cook over medium coals for 15-18 minutes, turning halfway. The foil traps steam, which helps the dough cook through, but you won't get the crispy exterior. It's a trade-off, but it's perfect for when you're juggling other camp tasks.</p>

<h2>Common Mistakes to Avoid</h2>

<p>The biggest mistake is overfilling. Trust me, I've done it—the pinwheels unroll and turn into a cheesy mess. Stick to the ratios above. Another mistake is cooking over too high heat. If the fire is roaring, wait until the coals are glowing and ash-covered. And always let the pinwheels rest for 2 minutes after cooking. This lets the cheese set and prevents the filling from oozing out when you bite into them.</p>

<h2>Make-Ahead and Storage Tips</h2>

<p>You can assemble the pinwheels at home and keep them in a sealed container in the cooler for up to 24 hours. Keep them cold below 40°F (4°C) until you're ready to cook. If you're using perishable fillings like cooked meat, make sure they reach an internal temperature of 165°F (74°C) during cooking. Don't leave raw dough or perishable fillings out in the danger zone (40°F-140°F) for more than 2 hours.</p>

<p>Leftover cooked pinwheels can be stored in the cooler and reheated in a skillet over low heat or in foil over coals. They're not as crisp the second time, but they're still tasty.</p>

<h2>Allergen Notes</h2>

<p>This recipe contains gluten, dairy, and possibly soy (in pepperoni). For a dairy-free version, use vegan cheese and skip the Parmesan. For gluten-free, use a gluten-free pizza dough and adjust cooking time. For a vegetarian version, swap pepperoni for extra veggies or plant-based pepperoni. If you have specific dietary needs, consult a qualified professional for personalized advice.</p>

<h2>Final Thoughts</h2>

<p>Pizza pinwheels are one of those camp meals that feel like a treat but come together with minimal fuss. The key is to respect the dough—don't overfill, roll tight, and cook over medium heat. Once you've got the technique down, you can experiment with different fillings and cooking methods. Whether you're feeding a crowd or just your family, these spirals are sure to be a hit.</p>

<h2>Frequently Asked Questions</h2>
<h3>Can I use pre-made pizza dough from the store?</h3>
<p>Yes, any refrigerated pizza dough works. Just let it come to room temperature for easier rolling. If using fresh dough from a bakery, you may need to let it rest for 30 minutes before rolling.</p>
<h3>How do I keep the pinwheels from burning on the bottom?</h3>
<p>Use medium heat, not high. If the skillet is too hot, the bottoms will burn before the dough cooks through. You can also place a heat diffuser or a layer of coals to the side for indirect heat. For foil packets, make sure to turn them halfway.</p>
<h3>Can I make these vegetarian or with different fillings?</h3>
<p>Absolutely. Swap pepperoni for sautéed mushrooms, spinach, or plant-based pepperoni. Keep the sauce and cheese ratio the same. For a white pizza version, use ricotta and garlic instead of tomato sauce.</p>
